How FD-1 is measured: seven benchmarks, one harness, no panel averages

FD-1 is our decision model: text in, a decision out, with a confidence score and an escalation flag. This note states exactly how its published figures were produced — the benchmarks, the sample sizes, the seed, and the calibration policy — and then gives the results, including the ones that are not flattering.

A benchmark figure is only as good as the protocol behind it. The decision-API market mostly publishes panel averages: one number, averaged over a private selection of tests, under a protocol the reader cannot inspect. Those numbers cannot be compared to each other, and they cannot be reproduced. We publish ours differently — per benchmark, with the protocol stated — and this note is the protocol.

The setup.

The model under test is FD-1 in its Tier-1 configuration, the configuration the public demo runs. It was run, unchanged, over seven public benchmarks chosen to cover the three decision shapes the API serves: entailment, yes/no, and multiple choice.

Every figure comes from the same harness, the same items, a single fixed seed (7), and no task-specific training — every panel is zero-shot. Each benchmark contributes 300 items, except MMLU, where the run covers the entire validation pool of six subjects — 228 items, named in the results table below. The harness’s exact variant of each benchmark (its task form, split, and scoring rule) is stated per benchmark on the benchmarks page; where a variant differs from the canonical task — MNLI run as a binary decision, TruthfulQA with sampled distractors — the difference is stated, never smoothed over. There is no per-benchmark tuning: a benchmark the model does badly on is reported exactly as it came out. Chance on a k-way choice is 1/k (TruthfulQA’s option count varies by item, so its sample chance level is 0.277), and the benchmarks page draws that line on every chart, so a score can be read against guessing, not just against other vendors.

Two numbers per benchmark.

We report accuracy and expected calibration error (ECE) together, always. Accuracy says how often the decision was right. ECE says whether the confidence meant what it claimed: predictions are grouped into 10 equal-width confidence bins, each bin’s mean confidence is compared with its empirical accuracy, and ECE is the weighted average gap. For multiple-choice panels the confidence is the winning option’s probability — top-label ECE, the standard form. A model can be accurate and still badly calibrated — confident exactly when it is wrong — and for a decision that triggers an action, the second failure is the expensive one.

Our calibration policy is strict: ECE is reported uncalibrated, at temperature 1.0 — the model’s raw confidence, before any rescaling. A rescaled figure would look better on several panels below. We do not publish rescaled calibration as the model’s calibration, because the number a caller’s system experiences, if they read our confidence at all, should be the number we measured, not the number we could manufacture.

Read honestly, this is a mixed table, and we would rather explain it than average it away. On MNLI — the entailment task closest to the decisions FD-1 is built for — accuracy is 0.920 with an ECE of 0.057: when the model says 80%, it is right about 80% of the time. That MNLI figure is a binary variant — neutral pairs are excluded, so the task is entailment-vs-contradiction against a 50% chance line, not the canonical three-way task. On AG News it is right about half the time, with calibration to match (ECE ≈ 0.30): usable signal, honestly labelled as uncertain. BoolQ needs its context stated: the sample runs 60% yes, so an always-yes baseline scores 0.600 — and FD-1’s 0.530 sits below it. On Banking77, 0.213 accuracy under strict exact match across all 77 intents is far above the 1-in-77 chance line and far below anything we would automate on its own. On HellaSwag and MMLU the model is near chance, and TruthfulQA’s 0.317 sits only modestly above its 0.277 sample chance level — and on all three, ECE near 0.5 says the model’s confidence there does not track its accuracy at all. Those three panels are the strongest argument in this note: a decision API that knows its confidence is meaningless on a class of inputs must say so, per call. Ours does, through the escalation flag, which the companion note covers.

WHAT WE WON’T CLAIM

That this table ranks FD-1 against any other product. The benchmarks page now sets every published decision-model figure for these seven benchmarks beside FD-1’s — but each in its own benchmark’s small table or graph, labelled with who measured it and under what protocol, because a figure without its protocol is not a comparison. We compare decision models with decision models; general-purpose LLMs are a different category and are not charted there. If a shared protocol ever exists — same items, same harness, every product — we will publish that comparison and let it say what it says.

Limitations.

Three, stated plainly. First, resolution: at n=300, a percentage point is three items; treat the second decimal as a reading, not a verdict. Second, one seed and one run: seed 7 is fixed and published so the figures are reproducible, not because it is the kindest draw. Third, these are public benchmarks — proxies for the decisions callers actually send, not measurements of them. A panel score tells you how the model behaves on a known distribution; your distribution is yours, which is why the demo runs the real model on your text.

And the variants themselves, since a variant is a limitation when it differs from the canonical task: MNLI is run as a binary decision with neutral pairs excluded, not the standard three-way classification. TruthfulQA’s distractors are sampled — at most three incorrect answers per item — rather than the full choice set canonical MC1 scores. MMLU’s 228 items are a six-subject convenience pool on the validation split (miscellaneous alone is 37.7% of it), not a representative draw from the 57-subject test set. BoolQ’s sample is unbalanced because its source split is; no balancing was applied. Each variant is labelled wherever its figure appears.

For serving context: median decision latency in the lab is 21 ms (p95 22.6 ms; GPU, n=200, batch 1, warm). And in the one vertical where FD-1 is task-trained rather than zero-shot — the spam demo vertical — the held-out error is 1.25% with ECE 0.046 (n=1,116, seed 42). The gap between that row and the zero-shot panels is the honest summary of this note: a decision model is strong where it has been trained and measured, and its published job everywhere else is to know the difference.

When the numbers change, we will update the benchmarks page and say so here. A lab notebook that only records the wins isn’t a lab notebook.
