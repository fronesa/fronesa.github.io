05 — Benchmark by benchmark

Every benchmark, side by side.

FD-1’s figures do not sit alone. Below, each of the seven benchmarks gets its own table: FD-1’s measured row first, with the exact variant of the task it ran stated above the table, then every figure the market has actually published for that benchmark — vendor-published, independently measured, and canonical reference results, each labelled as what it is, with its protocol beside it. Where a direct competitor publishes nothing for a benchmark, the table says so; silence is data, and it is never filled in by inference.

Nothing here is averaged across benchmarks, and nothing is ranked across different task forms. A fine-tuned specialist, a frontier model run few-shot, and a zero-shot decision API are three different kinds of measurement — the protocol column is what tells them apart.

Three different metrics circulate under this benchmark’s name, and they are never mixed here: MC1 (one true answer among 4–12, log-probability wins), MC2 (probability mass on all true answers — not an accuracy), and the binary setting introduced in January 2025 (two options, 0.50 floor). FD-1’s 0.317 is an MC1-style score against a 0.277 sample chance level; the binary panel’s 0.85–0.92 figures are a different, easier measurement on training-held-out subsets. Placing them in one ranking would manufacture a conclusion neither protocol supports.

FD-1’s pool is six of the 57 subjects on the validation split — miscellaneous alone is 37.7% of its items — against reference figures that are 5-shot on the full test set: different protocol classes, labelled rather than ranked. One figure is deliberately absent: Jev’s widely quoted 82.7% is an MMLU-Pro result (a different, harder benchmark — 10 options, different items, community-run) and it does not belong in an MMLU table. The GPT-4 calibration row is the one directly relevant precedent on this panel: a frontier base model exceptionally calibrated on MMLU-style items (0.007) whose post-training made it ≈10× worse (0.074) — published by its own vendor, and the standard for presenting uncalibrated ECE plainly.

How to read these tables

FD-1’s row is first in every table, and the exact variant it ran is stated above it — task form, split, sample size, scoring rule, seed. The other rows are the published record for that benchmark: most are fine-tuned specialists or frontier models run few-shot, which makes them ceilings and reference points, not peers; the rows that do share FD-1’s protocol class say so in their protocol cell. ECE appears only where a real figure was published with its method, and ECEs are never ranked — no two published ECEs in these tables share a dataset, a binning, and a confidence definition at the same time. Where a competitor publishes nothing, the table says “not published” and explains why. If a genuinely shared protocol ever exists — same items, same harness, every product — we will publish that comparison and let it say what it says.
